## Which speech/language tasks are most informative per minute of child time?

### Takeaway
Sentence repetition (`speak_this_line`) is the best-evidenced, most time-efficient single language measure available. It draws on morphosyntax, lexical phonology and verbal working memory, and a multilevel meta-analysis found a large group difference (about 2 SD) that holds across 19 languages and most task variations. Narrative retell gives rich data on expressive language and discourse, but it costs the most to score and needs a human or an LLM to score it. Confrontation naming and minimal-pair discrimination each answer a narrower question. `which_word` measures **speech perception / auditory discrimination, not articulation**, so it should be mapped to a different dimension.

### Cited Findings

**Sentence repetition (SR): `speak_this_line`**
- SR means listening to a sentence and repeating it verbatim with no delay. Sentences usually vary in length and/or complexity across the task. The task draws on short-term memory, prior language knowledge and conceptual representation of the sentence. — [Ward, Polišenská & Bannard 2024, JSLHR 67(7):2191–2221, accepted MS](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/32966/1/WARD%20et%20al%20-%20meta%20analysis.pdf)
- Polišenská et al. (2015, as cited in Ward et al.) found SR performance depends on the linguistic structure of the stimuli and is "primarily a test of lexical phonology and morphosyntax." — [Ward et al. 2024](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/32966/1/WARD%20et%20al%20-%20meta%20analysis.pdf)
- Meta-analysis scope (literature estimate): 3,459 records screened, 66 studies in the systematic review, 46 in the multilevel meta-analysis. These covered 19 languages, 39 different SR tasks and 4 main production-scoring methods. **Typically developing children outperformed children with DLD by 2.08 SD on average.** Effect size varied only with matching method and task language. The authors conclude SR's usefulness "appears robust to most kinds of task and study variation." — [Ward et al. 2024](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/32966/1/WARD%20et%20al%20-%20meta%20analysis.pdf)
- SR is described as quick and easy to administer. A scoping review (Rujas et al. 2021, cited in Ward) found 203 studies using SR from 2010–2021, of which 18% used it as a clinical marker. — [Ward et al. 2024](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/32966/1/WARD%20et%20al%20-%20meta%20analysis.pdf)
- A separate review snippet (JSLHR 2024 / ASHA Evidence Map) reported AUCs indicating good-to-excellent discrimination, especially in younger groups, but sensitivity/specificity fell below clinical thresholds in some samples. Supports use as a skill measure, not as a stand-alone classifier. — [ASHA Evidence Map summary](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/f849b558-e711-44c1-84fa-37353619497f); [JSLHR 2024 23-00490](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/full/10.1044/2024_JSLHR-23-00490) (snippet only, full text 403)
- LITMUS (COST Action IS0804) includes a cross-linguistic Sentence Repetition task and two language-neutral nonword repetition tasks designed for bilingual children. They are built to separate bilingual exposure effects from language impairment. — [PMC11202042 (Turkish/Swedish LITMUS study)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11202042/); [MAIN/LITMUS overview, ZAS](https://zaspil.leibniz-zas.de/article/view/606)

**Confrontation naming: `name_the_picture`**
- Computerised confrontation naming measures both naming latency and accuracy. Children with language impairment show longer latencies and more errors than typical peers. — [JSLHR 2010 09-0180](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2010/09-0180)) (snippet)
- In children with word-finding difficulties, naming *latency* was a significant impairment that was **not** related to standardised language measures. Latency therefore carries information that accuracy and standard tests miss. — [Open University, Language profiles and naming in children with WFD](https://oro.open.ac.uk/16570) (snippet)
- Naming errors are classified as semantic, formal (sound-related), mixed, nonword, unrelated, other. Semantic errors are most frequent in both typical and impaired groups. Formal and omission errors differ between groups. — [PolyU, Picture naming in children with and without SLI](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/picture-naming-in-children-with-and-without-specific-language-imp/) (snippet)

**Minimal-pair discrimination: `which_word`**
- Children with speech sound disorder (SSD) tend to have weaker speech perception than peers. Minimal-pair discrimination tasks in preschoolers showed lower scores in SSD groups. — [PMC7201332](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7201332); [JSLHR 2019 S-19-0127](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2019_JSLHR-S-19-0127) (snippets)
- **The direction of the perception–production relationship is unresolved.** Some SSD children can discriminate a contrast (e.g. /s/–/ʃ/) that they cannot produce, and others cannot. — [PMC7201332](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7201332) (snippet)
- Word-identification-with-minimal-pair-distractor designs ("Who is right?" style, picture-pointing) exist for young children, including in noise. — [Westminster Research](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/v7y5z/who-is-right-a-word-identification-in-noise-test-for-young-children-using-minimal-pair-distracters)

**Narrative retell: `retell_the_story`**
- Narratives are usually scored for macrostructure story-grammar elements: setting/introduction, initiating event, internal response, attempts/actions, outcome/ending. **Fiction *retell* scores separated age groups better, had higher reliability, and correlated more strongly with age and with another language measure** than the comparison condition. — [Springer, J Psycholinguist Res, s10936-023-10007-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-023-10007-y) (snippet)
- Narrative macrostructure shows strong age effects in preschoolers (Chinese sample). — [HKU 10722/173702](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/173702) (snippet)
- MAIN (LITMUS-MAIN) is a free, multilingual narrative instrument for ages 3–12, also used up to 17. It has **4 stories in 2 parallel sets, 6 pictures each**, and scores macrostructure (goal–attempt–outcome), microstructure and internal-state terms, plus 10 comprehension questions. — [MAIN overview, ZAS](https://zaspil.leibniz-zas.de/article/view/606); [Chan et al. 2020, PMC7714944, via EuropePMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7714944/fullTextXML)

**Clinical-group patterns: ADHD and autism**
- Autism: language phenotypes are highly heterogeneous, from nonverbal to above-average. A subgroup has structural language impairment ("ALI", Kjelgaard & Tager-Flusberg 2001, n=89, ages 4–14) and scored more than 1 SD below the mean on nonword repetition. Other autistic children have intact structure with mainly pragmatic differences. — [Tager-Flusberg, language phenotypes (BU)](https://bu.edu/autism/files/2010/03/T-F-language_phenotypes_CNS-20061.pdf); [Riches et al. 2011, nonword repetition in ASD (UCL)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10006783/1/Riches2011Non-word23.pdf); [Loucas 2010 (UCL)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10001419/1/Loucas2010Speech275.pdf)
- Autism narratives contain fewer micro- and macrostructure elements and are less coherent. Differences shrink when cognitive and linguistic demands are reduced. Narrative recall was predicted by short-term memory and nonverbal mental age, not expressive syntax. — [IMFAR 2016 abstract](https://imfar.confex.com/imfar/2016/webprogram/Paper21377.html); [Frontiers Psychol 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1402121/pdf) (snippets)
- ADHD: a meta-analysis (16 studies, n=1,015; coherence sub-analysis 9 studies, n=750) found youths with ADHD produce **less coherent narratives, medium effect size** (literature estimate). — [ADHD Evidence blog summary of Danish meta-analysis](https://adhdevidence.org/blog/meta-analysis-finds-narrative-language-impairment-in-youths-with-adhd) (secondary summary)
- ADHD narratives had lower scores specifically on "theme/topic" and "outcome" elements, with trouble keeping the central theme. This is attributed to executive function. — [CoDAS (SciELO)](https://scielo.br/j/codas/a/PK9GvdMh8gBLRHks9rcDP5M/?lang=en)
- Methylphenidate selectively improved story retelling in children with ADHD. Retell is therefore state/attention-sensitive, not a pure language trait measure. — [Bar-Ilan CRIS](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/methylphenidate-selectively-improves-story-retelling-in-children-/) (title/snippet)

### Inferences

**Information per minute (ranked; reasoning, not a published comparison):**
1. `speak_this_line` (SR): each trial takes a few seconds and gives one rich, graded response. It is a robust language marker across languages and scoring methods per Ward et al. Highest value per minute, **but it can only be scored once audio is scored** (ASR or human).
2. `which_word`: the fastest trials (one tap). It is fully scorable on-device now and needs no speech, so a shy or low-confidence child can still produce data. Its construct is narrow (perception of specific contrasts).
3. `name_the_picture`: fast trials. Latency is partly measurable without ASR. Accuracy and articulation need ASR or a human.
4. `retell_the_story`: costs the most child time (listen plus retell, roughly minutes per story) and the most scoring effort. It is the only task that taps discourse/macrostructure, which is the dimension where ADHD and autism differences show most.

**Per-task verdicts:**
- **`speak_this_line`: KEEP (week 1), capture-only scoring at first.** Use the SR paradigm (repeat a sentence verbatim). Recommended design (inference; the item counts below have **no literature estimate found** in this pass): about 10–16 sentences graded from short/simple (3–4 words, single clause) to long/complex (8–12+ words, embedded clauses, passives, negation, tense/plural morphology). Stop early after a run of low-scoring items so a 5-year-old is not pushed through items at ceiling difficulty. Use separate Urdu and English item sets built independently, **not translations** (LITMUS-SR builds language-specific sets for the same reason). Score per sentence with a graded 0–3 error-count scheme or proportion of words repeated correctly. Ward et al. found SR robust across the 4 scoring methods, so the cheaper method is acceptable.
- **`name_the_picture`: KEEP but narrow its purpose; consider MERGE with the articulation probe.** Its unique value is lexical retrieval (word-finding latency) plus a single-word articulation sample. Choose items so each target picture also elicits a planned target phoneme in a planned word position. One task then serves both `expressive_language` (retrieval) and `articulation`.
- **`which_word`: KEEP, but REMAP.** It measures speech-sound perception (phonological discrimination), not articulation. Map it to `auditory_comprehension`, or better, a new dimension such as `speech_perception`/`phonological_discrimination`. Feeding perception scores into `articulation` would wrongly raise or lower a production score, given the unresolved perception–production link (PMC7201332).
- **`retell_the_story`: KEEP in week 1 as capture-only, possibly DEFER scoring.** Record 1 story in week 1 (a second, parallel story at re-evaluation, MAIN-style parallel sets). Do not score until ASR plus an LLM or human rater pipeline is validated. In week 1 log only on-device features: response latency, speaking time, pauses, whether the child attempted at all. MAIN itself is copyright ZAS and free on registration. Use its *structure* (6-picture story, goal–attempt–outcome episodes) as a generic paradigm, but author our own stories, and check MAIN's licence before reusing any of its content.

**Should `name_the_picture` and `speak_this_line` both stay in week 1?** Yes, but keep each short. They measure different things: SR is morphosyntax plus verbal working memory, and naming is lexical retrieval plus single-word phonology. Both need audio, so for a child who declines the mic, both yield only "no response" signals. Make sure week 1 also includes `which_word` (no speech required) so every child gets *some* speech/language data. If week-1 time is tight, cut items per task rather than drop a task.

**Confounds to design around:**
- Shyness, low confidence and selective mutism-like behaviour reduce speech output. That is a willingness signal, not a language score. Log "no response" separately and never score it as 0 accuracy. It feeds `self_confidence`/comfort signals instead (rule #5).
- Echolalia (autism): in SR, an exact repetition is the correct response, so echolalia is not a confound for accuracy there. In naming and retell, delayed echolalic phrases or scripted speech can inflate word counts. A human/LLM retell scorer should mark verbatim chunks copied from the model story.
- ADHD: working-memory and attention lapses depress SR and retell. Short items, one practice item, repeat-on-request, and a visible progress cue reduce this. Results should be read alongside the focus tasks' signals, not in isolation.

### Gaps
- No pooled test–retest or split-half reliability figures for child SR, naming, minimal-pair or retell tasks were retrieved (full texts were blocked). The Ward et al. full results tables (scoring method × effect size, item counts) were not read beyond the abstract.
- No published evidence-based optimal item count for any of the four tasks was found in this pass. All item counts above are design inferences.
- No direct published comparison of "information per minute" across these four paradigms was found.
- Typical retell length (words, utterances, MLU) by age 5–12 was not found in sources retrieved here.
- No ADHD- or autism-specific picture-naming latency studies were found; search results covered SLI/word-finding difficulties only.

## What Urdu minimal pairs and Urdu speech-sound acquisition data exist?

### Takeaway
Urdu has phonemic aspiration (unaspirated vs aspirated stops, including breathy-voiced) and a phonemic dental vs retroflex place contrast. These are the best Urdu-specific minimal-pair contrasts and have no English equivalent. Urdu acquisition data exist but are thin, mostly preschool (about 2;7–6;0) and partly unpublished in mainstream journals. They show aspirated consonants and retroflexes developing late, around 4–5 years, and /ɽ/, /ɣ/ possibly not mastered until about 5–7. Cross-linguistically, 93% of consonants are correct by 5;0, with fricatives, affricates, trills and flaps last.

### Cited Findings
- **Urdu stop inventory**: voiceless/voiced × unaspirated/aspirated at bilabial (/p pʰ b bʱ/), dental (/t̪ t̪ʰ d̪ d̪ʱ/), retroflex (/ʈ ʈʰ ɖ ɖʱ/) and velar places. Aspiration and voicing are both phonemic. The dental–retroflex distinction is phonemic. — [Redalyc, Urdu stops phonology article](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/6638/663872611009/663872611009.pdf); [MIT fieldwork notes on Urdu](https://people.csail.mit.edu/namin/oldschool/linguistics/fieldwork1.pdf)
- Example Urdu minimal pairs from those sources: /sat̪/ 'seven' vs /sat̪ʰ/ 'together(ness)' (aspiration); /pal/ 'to raise' vs /bal/ 'hair' (voicing). — [Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/6638/663872611009/663872611009.pdf); [MIT fieldwork](https://people.csail.mit.edu/namin/oldschool/linguistics/fieldwork1.pdf)
- **Urdu acquisition (HKU project, typically developing Urdu-speaking children aged 2;7–6;0; JSLHR 2021 and HKU thesis/dataset "The development of speech sounds and phonetic complexity in Urdu-speaking children").** Reported patterns: girls acquire some consonants earlier than boys, voiced before voiceless, front before back place, stops first by manner, **aspirated consonants acquired last**. A systematic review in the same project found only **9 qualifying studies of Urdu speech-sound development over 40 years**. — [JSLHR 2021 21-00148](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-21-00148) (snippet; full text 403); [HKU hub 10722/350245](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/350245) (403); [HKU DataHub dataset](https://datahub.hku.hk/articles/dataset/Supporting_data_for_The_development_of_speech_sounds_and_phonetic_complexity_in_Urdu-speaking_children/22302226) (403)
- Secondary (practitioner site, lower quality): /p b t d k g m n/ reliable by about 2–3 years. Aspirated and retroflex consonants still developing at 4–5. /ɽ/ and /ɣ/ may not be mastered until about 5–7. — [Bilinguistics: Urdu speech and language development](https://bilinguistics.com/languages/urdu-speech-and-language-development/) (secondary, unverified against primary)
- A Pakistani-developed Urdu Speech sound Assessment Test (USAT) covers ages up to 8 and elicits all Urdu consonants and vowels in all word positions. — [J Riphah Coll Rehab Sci 2024](https://jrcrs.riphah.edu.pk/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/2024120104.pdf); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/30ce8ba3832244d9b4a94aa853b26e4a) (snippet; licence/proprietary status unknown, so do not copy its items)
- **Cross-linguistic norm (McLeod & Crowe 2018, AJSLP 27(4):1546–1571)**: 64 studies, 26,007 children, 27 languages (no Urdu/Hindi). Most consonants acquired by 5;0, with **at least 93% of consonants correct by 5;0** (literature estimate). Plosives and nasals earlier than **trills, flaps, fricatives and affricates**. — [AJSLP 2018](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2018_AJSLP-17-0100); [CSU record](https://researchoutput.csu.edu.au/en/publications/childrens-consonant-acquisition-in-27-languages-a-cross-linguisti/); [RCSLT summary PDF](https://rcslt.org/wp-content/uploads/media/Project/RCSLT/childrens-consonant-acquisition.pdf)
- **Urdu narrative**: an Urdu adaptation of MAIN (Hamdani, Kan, Chan, Gagarina, PolyU/ZAS) exists. It was built for Urdu-home / Cantonese-school bilinguals in Hong Kong and is "one of the first Indo-Aryan adaptations of MAIN". It accounts for Urdu SOV order, postpositions and no articles. Preliminary work suggests it separates children with and without language impairment. Protocols are free via ZAS registration, and the intro articles are CC-BY. — [Chan et al. 2020, EuropePMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7714944/fullTextXML)
- **Urdu ASR (adult)**: the first benchmark of Urdu ASR ("WER We Stand", 2024) notes limited representation due to dialect variety, **code-switching** and sparse data. Whisper-large was best on conversational speech and Seamless-large best on read speech. In a low-resource fine-tuning setting, Whisper-Small reached **33.68% WER**, versus Base 53.67% and Tiny 67.08% (literature estimates, adult speech). — [arXiv 2409.11252](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.11252)

### Inferences
- **Recommended Urdu contrasts for `which_word`** (from inventory facts; the item lists still need native-speaker and SLP review and picture-ability checks):
  - Aspiration: /p/–/pʰ/, /t̪/–/t̪ʰ/, /k/–/kʰ/ (e.g. the /sat̪/–/sat̪ʰ/ type, though "togetherness" is hard to picture, so pick imageable nouns).
  - Dental vs retroflex: /t̪/–/ʈ/, /d̪/–/ɖ/.
  - Voicing: /p/–/b/ (/pal/–/bal/ 'hair' is imageable for /bal/).
  - English set (for English-dominant children): voicing (pea/bee), place, and fricative/affricate (ship/chip, /s/–/ʃ/).
  - Avoid English contrasts that Pakistani English does not maintain (likely /v/–/w/, /θ/–/t̪/, /ð/–/d̪/; inference, not sourced here). Otherwise accent is scored as error, which breaks the constraint.
- **Age-gating for articulation scoring (inference from the sources above)**:
  - 5–7 band: do not count as errors aspirated stops, retroflexes, /ɽ/, /ɣ/, other fricatives/affricates, trills/flaps (Urdu /r/ is a trill or tap) and late English sounds. Score them as "emerging", with low weight.
  - 8–10: most consonants expected. Residual late-sound errors are low weight.
  - 11–12: near ceiling on single-word articulation. Use SR and retell for sensitivity, because naming accuracy will be at ceiling.
- **Bilingual and code-switching rule**: a correct word in the *other* language (e.g. English "car" in an Urdu naming trial) counts as a correct retrieval for `expressive_language` and is tagged `language=other`. Code-switching inside SR or retell is not an error. Only accuracy within the attempted language counts. Baseline-referencing (rule #3) also absorbs each child's language-dominance profile.
- **Children's Urdu ASR is effectively unavailable**: adult Urdu WER is already about 34% at the small-model tier, and child speech adds a further penalty. Automated phoneme scoring in Urdu is not feasible in the near term without a custom collected and annotated child corpus.

- Could not read the full HKU Urdu acquisition paper, so there are no per-consonant ages of acquisition at 75%/90% criteria and no PCC-by-age figures. The 5–7 year /ɽ/ /ɣ/ claim comes from a secondary site only. **Priority follow-up**: obtain JSLHR 2021 21-00148 and the HKU thesis.
- No Urdu acquisition data above about age 6–8 were found. No acquisition data for Urdu-English bilinguals in Pakistan specifically were found.
- No published Urdu minimal-pair perception task for children was found. No culturally normed Urdu picture-naming item set (name agreement, familiarity, age of acquisition) was found. Naming items must be piloted for name agreement in Pakistan (e.g. roti, chappal, rickshaw, cricket bat, mango).

## What can be measured reliably on a tablet without ASR?

### Takeaway
Measurable now without ASR: `which_word` tap accuracy and response time (fully deterministic), plus response latency / voice onset, speaking duration, pause counts and attempt-vs-no-response for every audio task (via on-device voice activity detection). Needs ASR plus a human or LLM: SR accuracy, naming accuracy, phoneme errors, retell content/macrostructure and MLU. Child ASR error rates are still high even in English (about 9–15% WER for read speech after fine-tuning, about 31–34% for spontaneous speech) and worse for non-native and Urdu speech. Recommended approach: capture audio now (consent-gated, rule #6), score later, with confidence capped low until human-validated.

### Cited Findings
- Whisper on child speech (English, MyST dataset): WER fell from 13.93% to 9.11% (Whisper-Small) and from 13.23% to 8.61% (Whisper-Medium) after fine-tuning (literature estimates). — [arXiv 2309.07927](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.07927)
- Primary-aged (about 7–11) read speech: Whisper WER 14.8%. Secondary (12–16) spontaneous speech: 30.7% native, 33.8% non-native. Read speech in secondary children: 8% native vs **24.8% non-native** (literature estimates). — [Univ. Galway, native and non-native child speech with Whisper](https://research.universityofgalway.ie/en/publications/exploring-native-and-non-native-english-child-speech-recognition-/)
- STT models including Whisper show substantial transcription errors on disordered paediatric speech. ASR is least reliable exactly where articulation errors exist. — [ICLR-W 2025, Adeyeye-Kayode et al.](https://mlanthology.org/iclrw/2025/adeyeyekayode2025iclrw-evaluating)
- Child ASR is hard because of high acoustic variability and scarce child data, especially for accented or low-resource languages. — [arXiv 2406.13431](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.13431); [Kid-Whisper, AAAI/AIES](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/31618)
- Adult Urdu ASR WER is about 34% even after low-resource fine-tuning of Whisper-Small. — [arXiv 2409.11252](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.11252)
- Naming latency carries independent information (word-finding difficulty not captured by standard measures). Latency is therefore worth capturing even before accuracy can be scored. — [Open University ORO 16570](https://oro.open.ac.uk/16570)
- SR is robust across scoring methods (4 main methods across 39 tasks), so a coarse scoring scheme does not destroy validity. — [Ward et al. 2024](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/32966/1/WARD%20et%20al%20-%20meta%20analysis.pdf)

### Inferences

**Scorable now, by task:**
| Task | Scorable now (no ASR) | Needs ASR + LLM/human |
|---|---|---|
| `which_word` | tap correct/incorrect, RT, per-contrast accuracy (e.g. aspiration vs retroflex), replay-requests | none |
| `name_the_picture` | voice-onset latency (VAD), attempt vs no-response, duration, hint use | accuracy, language used, semantic/phonological error type, target-phoneme correctness |
| `speak_this_line` | onset latency, utterance duration relative to model duration (crude completeness proxy), attempt rate, repeat requests | words/morphemes correct, error count per sentence |
| `retell_the_story` | onset latency, total speaking time, pause count/length, attempt | story-grammar elements, internal-state terms, MLU, coherence, copied (echoed) chunks |

**Phased plan:**
- **Phase A (now, week 1)**: emit all on-device signals as events. Store audio via `POST /media/upload-url` only with audio consent. Write `skill_scores` only for `which_word` (perception) and only as latency/engagement evidence for the others. Week 1 stays agent-free and deterministic.
- **Phase B**: human rater (SLP or trained rater) scores a sample of recordings with a non-proprietary rubric. This gives ground truth and inter-rater reliability.
- **Phase C**: ASR (English first, where WER is lower) plus LLM scoring of SR and retell, validated against Phase B ratings. Scores are marked `source=agent`/provisional per the existing decision log until agreement is shown. Urdu automated scoring is last, pending a child Urdu corpus.

**0–100 score + 0–1 confidence mapping (design proposal, no literature source):**
- `which_word`: score = 100 × proportion correct after correcting for chance (2-choice chance = 50%: (p − 0.5)/0.5, floored at 0), computed within age-appropriate contrasts. Confidence rises with the number of trials (as in the existing `stars_not_clouds` confidence-by-trial-count pattern) and falls if the RT pattern suggests random tapping (very fast uniform RTs).
- Latency-only evidence (naming/SR/retell in Phase A): do not convert to an `expressive_language` score. Store it as engagement/fluency evidence. If any score is written, cap confidence at about ≤0.3 so `p.low()` (confidence ≥0.5) cannot fire on it.
- SR (Phase C): score = mean per-sentence proportion of words correct, length-weighted. Confidence = f(n items scored, ASR confidence, rater agreement), capped (e.g. ≤0.5) until human-validated.
- Retell: macrostructure score = elements present / elements possible per story. Confidence is capped low because there is 1 story only in week 1.
- No-response trials are excluded from accuracy denominators and reported as comfort signals (rule #5). If too few responded trials remain, confidence collapses rather than the score dropping.

**Recommended week-1 structure (design inference; numbers not literature-sourced):** `which_word` about 16–24 trials across 3–4 contrasts, presented in the child's dominant language with a second-language set optional. `name_the_picture` about 15–20 items covering target phonemes in initial/medial/final positions. `speak_this_line` about 10–14 graded sentences with early stop. `retell_the_story` 1 short 5–6-picture story (goal–attempt–outcome episode structure modelled on MAIN's paradigm, original content). All prompts are pre-recorded human voice with picture support. Replay is allowed and logged. There is no error feedback, only effort praise.
