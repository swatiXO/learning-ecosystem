## 1. At what age do children's self-reports become reliable, and which formats work best for 5–7-year-olds?

### Takeaway
Self-report below ~5 is not valid; 5–7-year-olds can answer with pictorial, very few-option formats (ideally 2–3 choices, read aloud), but their answers are skewed toward the positive extreme and they struggle with middle options; from ~7–8 children can use 4–5-point (faces/Likert) scales with construct validity, and from ~8 self-evaluations become more realistic because social comparison comes online. For this app, about_me and mood_check_in self-reports in the 5–7 band should be treated as low-confidence context, not as precise scores.

### Cited Findings
**Age thresholds and response formats (general child self-report)**
- A systematic review of recall period, response-scale format and administration modality for child/young-person self-reported health outcomes concluded that children under 5 cannot validly and reliably self-report. — [Coombes et al., Quality of Life Research 2021, systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8233251/) (LIT: <5 years)
- Same review: children aged 7 and younger tend to think dichotomously and need two response options. — [Coombes et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11136-021-02814-4) (LIT: ≤7 years → 2 options)
- Same review: face scales showed better psychometric properties than visual-analogue or verbal Likert scales; from age 7, six-point faces scales showed convergent and discriminant validity. — [Coombes et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8233251/) (LIT: 7+ → up to 6 faces)
- Same review: for 4–7-year-olds, faces scales showed convergent validity with other formats, but scores were skewed, suggesting children answer at the extremes and cannot use the middle option. — [Coombes et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8233251/)
- Same review: most studies showed children from 8 can understand and use a 4- or 5-point Likert scale; reliability increases with age; younger children are more likely than older ones to respond positively and to skip items. — [Coombes et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8233251/) (LIT: 8+ → 4–5 points)
- Smiley-face Likert scales have been specifically studied for use with children (e.g. "Five Degrees of Happiness" in child–computer interaction), and the "Smileyometer" vs. "This or That" (paired forced choice) methods have been compared for preschool product-liking. — [Hall, Hume & Tazzyman 2016 (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/305726958_Five_Degrees_of_Happiness_Effective_Smiley_Face_Likert_Scales_for_Evaluating_with_Children); [Zaman et al. on Smileyometer vs This-or-That](https://www.academia.edu/11829703/Measuring_product_liking_in_preschool_children_An_evaluation_of_the_Smileyometer_and_This_or_That_methods)
- Validity concerns about pictorial Likert scales have been raised as a distinct literature (pictures can carry meaning that differs from the anchor words). — [Reynolds-Keefer & Johnson, "Validity Issues in the Use of Pictorial Likert Scales" (Semantic Scholar)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Validity-Issues-in-the-Use-of-Pictorial-Likert-Reynolds-Keefer/f9f1c6f3e6b12f23041709bf4b251fad5be77e6f)

**Developmental positivity in self-concept**
- In early childhood, self-statements are extremely positive with an inflated sense of self; young children compare their present ability with what they could do earlier (temporal comparison) rather than with peers. — [Leiden University thesis summarising Harter's developmental model](https://scholarlypublications.universiteitleiden.nl/access/item%3A3185684/download); [Belsky, Experiencing the Lifespan ch. 6](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/belsky4e/belsky4e_ch6_3.html)
- Around age 8 social comparison becomes a tool for self-evaluation and self-appraisal becomes more accurate; self-esteem tends to decline across early elementary school as a result. — [Belsky, Experiencing the Lifespan ch. 6](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/belsky4e/belsky4e_ch6_3.html) (LIT: ~8 years)
- The Harter–Pike Pictorial Scale of Perceived Competence and Social Acceptance was built specifically for ages 4–7 using pictures and concrete behaviours; its 24 items (4 a-priori subscales) empirically collapse to two factors, General Competence and Social Acceptance — i.e. young children do not differentiate fine-grained self-concept domains. — [PMC5969031 (Polish/other validation context)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969031); [EdInstruments entry](https://edinstruments.org/node/605) (proprietary instrument — format described only, no items used)

**The "some kids… other kids" (structured alternative) format**
- Harter's structured-alternative format ("Some kids … but other kids …", child picks which kind of kid they are like, then "really true / sort of true") was designed to reduce socially desirable responding. — [Education Endowment Foundation measures database](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/measures-database/harters-self-perception-profile-for-children)
- Its reliability/validity in middle childhood is "still under debate"; a multigroup CFA comparison found the Harter format and a conventional format had comparable reliability and factorial validity, with only a slight advantage for Harter's format on one scale. — [Univ. Padova record of format-comparison study](https://research.unipd.it/handle/11577/3292128)
- Contradicting the rationale: a meta-analysis of 11 experiments with adolescents found the some/other form increased reports of undesirable attributes but produced **lower criterion validity** than direct questions, took longer and required more cognitive effort; the authors recommend avoiding it. — [Yeager & Krosnick, Developmental Psychology (Stanford PDF)](https://web.stanford.edu/dept/communication/faculty/krosnick/docs/2011/Some%20other%20adolescents%20-%20Developmental%20Psych%20PUBLISHED.pdf) (note: adolescent sample, not 5–12)

**Mood self-report / single items**
- An EMA smartphone app for 4–10-year-olds (n=20, mean age 7.7) using a talking cartoon character, thumbs-up/down and 1–5 visual scales achieved ~89% valid response rate; parent–child agreement r=0.89–0.97 — but that agreement was on behavioural/contextual questions (e.g. location), not on mood, and no test–retest or per-age reliability was reported; older children (8–10) found audio narration too slow. — [Building an EMA smartphone app for 4–10-year-olds, PMC10468030](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10468030/) (LIT: 89% compliance)
- An 8-item pictorial well-being scale (EMOWI) showed α=0.85 and intraday test–retest ICC=0.83 — in adolescents, and as a multi-item scale, not a single item. — [search summary of EMOWI study](https://doaj.org/article/d6dd54f88d4d4317b52be527f33b94c3) (LIT; population mismatch)

**ADHD and autism confounds on self-report**
- Positive illusory bias (PIB): children with ADHD give very positive reports of their own competence relative to criteria of actual competence. — [Owens et al., critical review of PIB, Clin Child Fam Psychol Rev (mijn.bsl.nl)](https://mijn.bsl.nl/a-critical-review-of-self-perceptions-and-the-positive-illusory-/524570)
- Children with ADHD overestimated competence relative to teacher estimates in all domains more than non-ADHD children, on both explicit and implicit measures, while typically developing children tended to be accurate. — [Explicit and implicit PIB in children with ADHD (MetaTOC)](https://metatoc.com/papers/53160-explicit-and-implicit-positive-illusory-bias-in-children-with-adhd); [ADHD Atten Def Hyp Disord 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s12402-010-0043-x)
- PIB is not universal: cluster analysis found a positive-bias group (ADHD only), a negative-bias group (ADHD and controls), and a largest accurate group (both) — i.e. PIB is confined to a subgroup. — [ADHD Atten Def Hyp Disord 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s12402-010-0043-x)
- PIB applies to self only; children with ADHD rated others' competence no differently from controls. — [The positive illusory bias: do inflated self-perceptions generalize to perceptions of others? (FIU)](https://discovery.fiu.edu/display/pub241104)
- Alexithymia (difficulty identifying/describing one's own feelings) is substantially elevated in autism on both self- and parent-report. — [PMC9839896](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9839896)
- In 35 autistic and 40 non-autistic youth aged 8–17, the autistic group had higher alexithymia and physiological hyperarousal but no group difference in interoception or emotional empathy. — [Southampton eprint: Interoception and Emotion in Autistic and Non-autistic Children and Adolescents](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/492859/1/Interoception_and_Emotion_in_Autistic_and_Non-autistic_Children_and_Adolescents.pdf)
- Meta-analysis of 32 studies: alexithymia correlates positively with interoceptive confusion and negatively with interoceptive accuracy; ~25% of individual differences in emotion-relevant interoception were accounted for by alexithymia, across people with and without autism. — [search summary of systematic review/meta-analysis (DOAJ, PLoS ONE 2024)](https://doaj.org/article/7e53624b2a524e0d8678bb8079b66ec4); [City Univ. preprint, Gaigg, Cornell & Bird](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/15259/1/Gaigg%2C%20Cornell%20%26%20Bird%20%28prepub%29.pdf)

**Pakistan / Urdu localisation**
- An Urdu version of the Children's Emotion Management Scales was validated in Pakistani schoolchildren: cross-language correlation r=0.846–0.891 (n=169), internal consistency 0.808–0.904 (n=683), original factors retained. — [Baig et al., PMC11008575](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11008575/) (LIT; shows Urdu translation of child emotion constructs is feasible, using a forward/back-translation + bilingual cross-validation design)
- An Urdu translation of the Stirling Children's Emotional and Psychological Well-Being Scale was piloted in 150 urban Pakistani children aged 8–16 and found reliable and valid. — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2023 (fpsyt.2023.1214708)](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1214708/text) (LIT; note: 8+ only, urban, school-based — no Urdu validation evidence found for 5–7)

### Inferences

**Format rules for this app (applies to about_me and mood_check_in)**
- Age band 5–7: 2 options (or 3 with a clearly distinct middle), always audio-narrated in the child's home language, pictures carry the meaning; expect positive skew and treat answers as low-confidence. Based on Coombes et al. 2021 dichotomous-thinking finding (LIT ≤7 → 2 options).
- Age band 8–10: up to 4–5 faces/points. Based on Coombes et al. 2021 (LIT 8+ → 4–5 points).
- Age band 11–12: 5 points is safe; a sixth adds little for a tablet UI. DESIGN PROPOSAL.
- Narration speed should be adjustable or skippable for 8+ (EMA pilot found narration too slow for 8–10).
- Do **not** default to Harter's "some kids/other kids" format: evidence for its benefit is mixed (comparable to conventional format in one CFA; lower criterion validity in Yeager & Krosnick's adolescent meta-analysis), and it adds cognitive load — a particular concern for children with attention or language difficulties. A simpler "two-picture choice" (child taps the character who is more like them, no "some kids" preamble) keeps the forced-choice benefit with less verbal load. This is an inference; no study tested it in 5–7-year-olds.

**Activity: about_me — design notes**
- Construct: global/domain self-concept and self-efficacy beliefs. In 5–7s the literature suggests only two broad factors (general competence, social acceptance) are distinguishable (Harter–Pike factor structure), so do not try to score more than ~2 sub-domains in that band.
- Structure (DESIGN PROPOSAL): 6–8 original two-picture items, each a concrete behaviour rather than an abstract trait (e.g. a character who keeps trying a puzzle vs. one who stops; a character who puts a hand up to answer vs. one who waits quietly — illustrative, original, not drawn from any instrument), plus a reverse-keyed half so that "always pick left/top" or "always pick the happy-looking one" is detectable. Repeat the same item set at baseline (week 1) and at each 6–8-week re-evaluation; do not change items between occasions, or the baseline comparison breaks.
- Acquiescence/positivity check: count same-side or all-positive response runs; flag response sets as low-confidence rather than as low/high confidence. Pictures should be balanced so that the "low-confidence" option is not drawn as sad/bad (otherwise children pick the smiling one — the pictorial-validity issue raised by Reynolds-Keefer & Johnson).
- Scoring (DESIGN PROPOSAL): proportion of confidence-keyed choices → 0–100; confidence weight starts low and rises with age band (e.g. cap 0.3 for 5–7, 0.5 for 8–10, 0.7 for 11–12), reduced further if an acquiescence run is detected. These caps are not from literature; they encode the literature's qualitative finding that reliability increases with age.
- ADHD/autism: an extremely high about_me score combined with low persistence in oops_try_again is consistent with PIB in a subgroup, not proof of high confidence; for autistic children, items about feelings ("I feel proud when…") are more affected by alexithymia than items about concrete behaviour, so prefer behaviour items.
- Verdict: **REDESIGN** — keep as an occasion-level self-perception probe, but make it concrete-behaviour, two-picture, reverse-keyed, and down-weighted in 5–7; never the sole input to self_confidence.

**Activity: mood_check_in — design notes**
- Construct: momentary affect (valence; possibly arousal). Developmental literature supports faces scales, with 2–3 options at 5–7 and up to 5 at 8+ (Coombes et al. 2021).
- Mood-to-valence scale design (DESIGN PROPOSAL): store a band-independent valence on −2…+2 (or 0–100) so pre/post deltas are comparable across bands: 5–7 band → 3 faces mapped to {−2, 0, +2}... but because 5–7s avoid the middle option (Coombes et al. 2021), consider 2 faces {happy, not happy} plus an optional "a bit tired/worried" tap; 8–10 → 5 faces mapped −2…+2; 11–12 → 5 faces plus an optional arousal choice (calm ↔ excited/"jittery") as a second dimension. Faces must vary only in mouth/eyes, matched in size and colour, with neutral (not red/green) colouring.
- Reliability: I found no source reporting test–retest reliability of a single-item mood face rating in 5–12-year-olds. The closest data (EMA pilot) reported compliance and parent agreement on non-mood items; the EMOWI reliability is for a multi-item adolescent scale. So single-item mood in this age range should be assumed noisy.
- Autism: alexithymia is elevated, and interoceptive confusion tracks alexithymia, so a face choice may reflect a guess or a favourite face rather than felt state. ADHD: positive bias may extend to mood reporting (not directly evidenced — the PIB literature is about competence).
- Answer to question (b) — should mood_check_in be scored? **No, not as a skill score.** Use it as context: (1) a covariate/annotation on that session's activity scores (e.g. down-weight a session that starts "not happy"), (2) a delivery input (offer calm mode or a shorter session), (3) a pattern a human can review (persistently low mood over many sessions is a candidate signal for the safety-flag review queue under rule #8, after Phase 0 clinical input). Rationale: there is no validated mapping from single-item pictorial mood to a stable skill, its reliability in this age range is undocumented, and alexithymia in autism plus young-child positivity make it a poor ability measure. Pre→post deltas averaged over many sessions could be reported to parents descriptively ("most sessions end the same or happier"), never as a skill.
- Verdict: **KEEP as context, do not score** (drop it from the sensory_regulation evidence mapping it currently has in PROJECT.md §6).

**Localisation (both activities)**
- Use a forward translation → back-translation → bilingual cross-check process, as the Urdu CEMS validation did (Baig et al.), and pilot comprehension with 5–7-year-olds, because published Urdu validations found were 8+ and urban.
- Mood vocabulary should be concrete everyday words narrated aloud (inference: e.g. Urdu خوش "khush" happy, اداس "udaas" sad, غصہ "ghussa" angry, ڈر "dar" scared/afraid, تھکا ہوا "thaka hua" tired) — these are common Urdu words but I found no study validating a child mood-word list in Urdu; this is a gap and the list needs checking with Pakistani teachers/parents in Phase 0. Faces should be culturally neutral cartoons (non-religious, non-gendered, skin tone neutral or child-matched to avatar) — inference, not sourced.
- Urdu reads right-to-left: horizontal scales should be checked for direction effects (does "most positive" go on the left in Urdu UI?). No study found; flag for pilot.

### Gaps
- No source found giving test–retest reliability of single-item mood face ratings for 5–12-year-olds.
- No validated Urdu child mood vocabulary or Urdu faces scale for 5–7-year-olds found.
- No study found comparing the structured-alternative format with simple two-picture choice specifically in 5–7-year-olds, or in children with ADHD/autism.
- Could not find quantitative PIB meta-analysis effect sizes (searches returned reviews and single studies only).
- The Coombes et al. 2021 review concerns health outcomes (pain, quality of life); generalisation to self-concept/mood is an inference.

## 2. What does persistence-after-setback research measure, and how is it operationalised in children?

### Takeaway
Persistence-after-setback paradigms (Dweck tradition) measure a motivational pattern — mastery vs. helpless response — operationalised as task choice (learning vs. performance goal), desire/time to persist after an engineered difficult set, enjoyment, and attributions; laboratory versions used deliberately too-hard problems followed by a debrief and a success experience. In ADHD, quitting after difficulty is strongly tied to low frustration tolerance, so in-app retry/quit behaviour is a mixed signal of confidence, frustration tolerance, boredom and attention — defensible as one input to self_confidence only when combined with other evidence and compared to the child's own baseline.

### Cited Findings
**Mueller & Dweck 1998 paradigm (the canonical one)**
- Sample (Study 1): 128 fifth graders, ages 10–12 (mean 10.7). Task: three sets of 10 Raven Standard Progressive Matrices, 4 minutes each. Set 1 moderate difficulty; all children told they did very well (at least 80% correct regardless of actual score) plus intelligence, effort, or neutral praise. Set 2 was much harder (later revealed to be seventh-grade level) and followed by negative feedback; set 3 moderate again. — [Mueller & Dweck 1998, JPSP 75(1):33–52](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf) (LIT: 10 items/set; 4 min/set; ages 10–12)
- Task choice measure: after set 1, children chose among four kinds of problems — three performance-goal options (easy/not-too-hard/show-I'm-smart) and one learning-goal option (learn a lot even if I won't look smart); three performance options were included to offset the social desirability of the learning option. Choices were placed in an envelope so experimenters stayed blind. — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)
- Result: 67% of intelligence-praised children chose performance goals vs 8% of effort-praised (92% of effort-praised chose learning goals); control split roughly evenly. Replicated in Study 2 (69% vs 12%) and Study 3 (55% vs 23%, control 34%). — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf) (LIT)
- Persistence was a self-reported intention, not behaviour: "How much would you like to take these problems home to work on?" rated 1–6. Post-failure persistence: intelligence-praised M=3.25 (SD 1.41) vs effort M=4.53 (SD 1.03) vs control M=4.30 (Study 1); Study 3: 3.24 vs 5.20 vs 4.28. Enjoyment on 1–6 showed the same ordering. — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf) (LIT)
- Importantly, Study 2 showed **no** differences in persistence or enjoyment after success alone — differences emerge only after a challenge. — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)
- Attributions were measured with a four-colour adjustable disk (effort / ability ×2 / time), scored as the proportion of 36 segments. — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)
- Ethics: in debriefing all children were told set 2 was meant for older children and that solving even one was an achievement; "extensive precautions" ensured children left proud. — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)
- Younger children: Kamins & Dweck (1997, cited in Mueller & Dweck) found kindergarten-age children given person/trait feedback showed more self-blame, negative affect and low persistence after setbacks than those given strategy feedback — evidence the pattern exists by ~5–6. — [cited in Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)
- Replication dispute: Li & Bates (2019), 3 studies, 624 individually tested 10–12-year-olds, found intelligence praise did not harm cognitive performance and mindset was unrelated to IQ or grades; Dweck & Yeager (2019) responded that the replication deviated from protocol and that corrected analyses corroborated the original. — [Wikipedia summary of the exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck); [Li & Bates preprint (gwern)](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/2020-li.pdf) (the effect size of praise on persistence is contested; treat it as directional, not a fixed number)

**Task choice / risk-taking (Atkinson tradition) — relevant to level_choice**
- Atkinson's risk-taking model: people with dominant motive for success prefer intermediate difficulty; those with dominant motive to avoid failure choose very easy or very hard tasks. — [Psychological Reports 1996](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.2466/pr0.1996.78.3c.1259); [Slade & Rush, JPSP 1991 (PDF)](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Slade-JP&SP91.pdf)
- Preschool children chose easy tasks when their goal was favourable evaluation by others, intermediate tasks when interested in the task, and difficult tasks when focused on the value of success — i.e. task choice reflects goals, not only confidence. — [Acta Psychologica Sinica 2004, 36(6):744](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/acps/EN/Y2004/V36/I06/744)
- Both motive groups shift toward harder tasks over repeated trials, with success-motivated individuals shifting faster — so choice has a learning/practice drift that needs a baseline. — [Slade & Rush 1991](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Slade-JP&SP91.pdf)

**ADHD confounds: frustration tolerance**
- Youth with ADHD were more likely to quit a frustration-tolerance task (Mirror Tracing Persistence Task, a validated computerised measure) than youth without ADHD; low frustration tolerance was linked to ADHD itself and not better explained by ODD. — [Seymour et al., Frustration Tolerance in Youth With ADHD, PubMed 27282378](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27282378/)
- Boys with ADHD gave up on significantly more puzzles, especially after an insolvable set, and reported more frustration; stimulant medication prevented the post-insolvable decrement. — [Milich & colleagues, UKnowledge psychology_facpub/25](https://uknowledge.uky.edu/psychology_facpub/25); [psychology_facpub/26](https://uknowledge.uky.edu/psychology_facpub/26)

### Inferences

**What in-app retry/quit actually measures**
- The Dweck-tradition measures (goal choice, persistence intention, enjoyment, attributions) are about motivational response to difficulty. A quit or a slow retry in oops_try_again can equally reflect: low confidence/helpless attribution; low frustration tolerance (strong ADHD evidence above); boredom/low interest (Acta Psychologica Sinica finding that interest drives intermediate choices implies disinterest drives disengagement); attention lapse; sensory discomfort; or simply wanting to move on. Rule #5 (quits are comfort signals) is consistent with this: a quit should not be read as "low confidence" without corroboration.

**Activity: oops_try_again — design notes**
- Ethical setback with no fail state (DESIGN PROPOSAL, modelled on Mueller & Dweck's "harder set then success" structure but softened): a short, engaging mini-task (e.g. stack/build/catch) where one round is quietly made harder (faster, one more piece) so the child is likely not to finish it. The game shows a neutral, playful outcome ("the tower wobbled! want another go?") — never a red X, sad sound or score drop. The child always sees two equally prominent options: "try again" and "do something else". Whatever they choose, they get an effort reward ("you kept going" / "good thinking to take a break"). The session always ends on a success round, mirroring Mueller & Dweck's debrief principle of leaving the child proud.
- Use effort/strategy feedback only after success (never "you're smart"), because ability praise is the condition that produced lower post-failure persistence (Mueller & Dweck) — note that using effort praise everywhere will shift every child's persistence upward, which is fine because scoring is baseline-relative, but the feedback wording must be held constant across occasions.
- Observables: retry vs. switch (binary), latency to decision (ms), number of voluntary retries before switching (bounded, e.g. max 3, DESIGN PROPOSAL), and an optional one-tap post-setback face ("how did that feel?") — skippable.
- Occasions (DESIGN PROPOSAL): at least 3 engineered setbacks across week 1 on different days and in 2 different mini-task skins, because a single trial is dominated by momentary state (frustration, boredom). No source gives a required number of occasions for a reliable child persistence score; this is a gap.
- Scoring (DESIGN PROPOSAL): persistence index = proportion of setbacks followed by a voluntary retry, with latency as a tie-breaker; map to 0–100; confidence = f(number of setback occasions, consistency), and down-weight occasions that coincide with a low mood_check_in or calm-mode activation (context, not score). Report change from the child's own baseline.
- ADHD/autism: high quit rates in ADHD are better explained by frustration tolerance than confidence (Seymour et al.); for autistic children, a quit may reflect a disrupted routine or sensory overload, and repeated retries may reflect perseveration rather than confidence (the perseveration concern is already noted in PROJECT.md §12; not separately sourced here).
- Verdict: **KEEP, with REDESIGN** — keep the behavioural measure (it is the most behavioural of the three confidence inputs), but rename the construct internally as "persistence after difficulty" evidence feeding self_confidence, require multiple occasions, and never treat a single quit as low confidence.

**Activity: level_choice — design notes**
- Construct: challenge-seeking relative to demonstrated skill — an adaptation of the Mueller & Dweck goal-choice measure plus Atkinson risk preference. Key design point from both literatures: choice must be interpreted **relative to the child's actual success rate** at that level (a child who picks "easy" when they succeed 40% of the time at medium is calibrated, not unconfident).
- Structure (DESIGN PROPOSAL): offer a three-door choice (easy / medium / tricky — framed as "practice", "fun", "big challenge", never "hard" in a threatening sense) at the start of several rounds across week 1 (e.g. 4–6 choice points), with levels pre-calibrated per age band. Follow Mueller & Dweck's design trick of offsetting social desirability: the "big challenge" option must not be visually privileged (no extra glow or bigger reward), and rewards must be equal for all choices (effort-based), so choice reflects preference rather than reward-seeking. Record the child's recent success rate at each level alongside each choice.
- Scoring (DESIGN PROPOSAL): challenge index = mean of (chosen level − level the child's demonstrated success rate supports), centred so that "chooses about at or slightly above demonstrated level" scores highest, and extreme under-choice and extreme over-choice both score lower (Atkinson: avoidance-motivated people choose very easy *or* very hard). Map to 0–100; confidence rises with number of choice points. Track drift toward harder choices over time as normal (Slade & Rush 1991) and compare against own baseline only.
- Confounds: young children's choices are driven by goals such as pleasing the adult, interest, or the value of success (Acta Psychologica Sinica 2004), and PIB in an ADHD subgroup may produce over-choice that looks like confidence. 5–7s may also pick by colour/position — counterbalance door positions.
- Verdict: **KEEP, with REDESIGN** to score calibration (choice vs. demonstrated skill) rather than raw "picked hard".

**Answer to question (c): is a self_confidence score built from about_me + oops_try_again + level_choice defensible?**
- **Defensible as a provisional, baseline-relative composite — not as a stand-alone or cross-child measure — under these conditions** (inference from the evidence above):
  1. The three inputs map onto distinct, established facets: self-perception (about_me), response to difficulty (oops_try_again; Mueller & Dweck persistence), and challenge-seeking (level_choice; Mueller & Dweck goal choice, Atkinson). Using three facets with different method biases (self-report positivity vs. behavioural frustration tolerance vs. choice/goal effects) is better than any single one.
  2. Weight behavioural inputs above self-report in the 5–7 band and for any child whose about_me shows acquiescence or a large gap from behaviour (PIB pattern). DESIGN PROPOSAL: e.g. about_me 20%, oops_try_again 40%, level_choice 40% in 5–7; 33/33/33 in 11–12.
  3. Store each facet separately in `evidence` and show the facets to adults, not only the composite (rule #7), because a low composite driven entirely by quits may be a frustration-tolerance or attention issue — a different module (focus_attention) may be more appropriate.
  4. Label it "confidence & persistence" (as PROJECT.md §6 already does), never a trait of the child.
  5. Validate against a teacher/parent rating in the pilot before it drives plan weighting strongly; no source was found validating any app-based composite of this kind in 5–12-year-olds.
- Weakness to state plainly: the Mueller & Dweck effect itself is contested (Li & Bates 2019 vs Dweck & Yeager 2019), and its persistence measure was a self-rated intention, not behaviour, so the app's behavioural operationalisation is an extension, not a replication.

### Gaps
- No source found for the minimum number of setback occasions or choice points needed for a reliable persistence/challenge score in children.
- No behavioural (time-based) persistence norms for 5–7 vs 8–10 vs 11–12; Mueller & Dweck sampled only 9–12-year-olds; Kamins & Dweck (kindergarten) known only via citation.
- No direct evidence found on autistic children in Dweck-style setback paradigms.
- No study found that separates boredom from low confidence as a cause of quitting in a tablet game.

## 3. What does the research say about measuring sensory processing via device behaviour?

### Takeaway
Sensory differences are common and real (strongest in autism, especially under-responsivity, and present in ~8–15% of typical children), but the research base for inferring sensory processing from tablet behaviour is thin and experimental (touch/tilt "digital markers" studies aimed at group discrimination, not individual skill measurement). Volume and calm-mode settings reflect preference, mood, environment (noisy home, shared device), adult choices and novelty as much as sensory processing; sensory_setup should be reframed as a comfort/preference setting that informs delivery, and sensory_regulation should be parked as a scored skill.

### Cited Findings
- Sensory over-responsivity (SOR) is a faster, more intense or longer response to sensation; it is more frequent in autism than in typical development. — [Interventions for SOR in ASD: narrative review, PMC9601143](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9601143/)
- In a survey of over 4,000 autistic youth, about six in ten currently showed auditory over-responsivity. — [Brief report: prevalence and severity of auditory SOR (bsl.nl)](https://antoniusacademie.mijn.bsl.nl/brief-report-prevalence-and-severity-of-auditory-sensory-over-re/19078432) (LIT: ~60% auditory SOR in autistic youth)
- SOR occurs in isolation in roughly 8–15% of typically developing children; in children with clinically significant anxiety, 93.2% were bothered by at least one tactile or auditory sensation. — [Diagnostic validity of SOR: literature review (sensoryhealth.org PDF)](https://sensoryhealth.org/sites/default/files/publications/DiagnosticvalidityofSOR.pdf) (LIT: 8–15% typical)
- The same review reportedly found SOR not directly related to ADHD (from search summary; I did not verify this in the full text). — [sensoryhealth.org PDF](https://sensoryhealth.org/sites/default/files/publications/DiagnosticvalidityofSOR.pdf)
- Meta-analysis of 14 studies (Ben-Sasson et al. 2009, JADD): large ASD vs typical difference in sensory symptoms, largest for under-responsivity, then over-responsivity, then sensation seeking; differences were largest at ages 6–9 and moderated by autism severity and control-group type. — [PubMed 18512135](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18512135/) (LIT: peak difference ages 6–9)
- Tablet task (14–68-month-olds, n=63): autistic children showed reduced relative preference for a social-reward button, measured as proportion of touches — an example of preference inferred from touch choices, but for social reward, not sensory processing. — [Scientific Reports 2017, PMC5468258](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5468258/)
- "Feel and Touch", a smartphone haptic game (83 Mexican children), identified three digital markers from tilt and touch patterns distinguishing autistic from neurotypical children — a group-discrimination study, not a validated individual sensory measure. — [Smartphone haptics touch-interaction differences, DOI 10.1145/3699749](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/smartphone-haptics-can-uncover-differences-in-touch-interactions-between-asd-and-neurotypicals)
- A review of serious games for autistic children with sensory processing difficulties notes limited design knowledge and states more research is needed on their efficacy. — [Systematic review background article (Zakari et al.)](https://gitfront.io/r/UnivaqRepository/Ld1cG22q1AKd/SystematicReview/raw/Background%20Articles/Zakari.pdf)

### Inferences

**Activity: sensory_setup — design notes**
- What the settings can and cannot tell us: a child who turns volume down and uses calm mode often may be sound/visually over-responsive — or in a noisy shared room, using a cheap tablet with harsh speakers, copying a sibling, told by a parent, tired, or exploring the toggle for fun (novelty toggling is especially likely in week 1). Under-responsivity — the largest autism difference in Ben-Sasson 2009 — would not show up as calm-mode use at all (if anything, as turning things up), so a calm-mode-rate score would systematically miss the most common pattern. This asymmetry is an inference from the meta-analysis, not a tested finding.
- None of the device-behaviour studies found measure sensory processing at the individual level against a validated reference, and those that exist aim to discriminate diagnostic groups — which would conflict with this app's rule #1 (no diagnosis labels) if repurposed.
- Structure (DESIGN PROPOSAL): Day-1 picture choices for sound (soft / medium / loud, with a live preview), animation (still / gentle / lively) and an always-visible calm button; re-offered briefly at each session start (one tap to keep). Log every change with context (session time, activity, mood_check_in answer) so a human can see patterns.
- Use: settings drive delivery immediately (volume, animation, calm mode default, shorter sessions). Patterns (e.g. calm mode turned on mid-activity in most sessions of one activity type) are surfaced to the guardian/therapist as a descriptive observation ("often turns sounds down during X"), consistent with PROJECT.md §12's "noticing a pattern and surfacing it to the guardian" role for routine_sensory.
- Localisation: Pakistani homes vary widely in ambient noise and device sharing; the guardian should be told in Urdu/English that settings are comfort choices, not tests (inference, no source).
- Verdict: **REFRAME and PARK the score** — keep sensory_setup as a comfort/preference setting and a logged context signal; do not compute a sensory_regulation skill score from it.

**Answer to question (a): can sensory_regulation be measured from calm-mode toggles and volume?**
- No — not meaningfully as a skill score for an individual child on current evidence. The construct (sensory over-/under-responsivity, sensation seeking) is well-established and prevalent, but (1) the device-behaviour literature is experimental and group-level, (2) the settings are confounded by environment, novelty, adult influence and mood, (3) calm-mode use cannot capture under-responsivity (the largest autism-vs-typical difference), and (4) "regulation" is not something preference toggling demonstrates — choosing a quieter setting is adaptive self-management, which a scored "low" would perversely penalise, conflicting with rule #5 (no fail states / comfort signals). Recommendation: park `sensory_regulation` as a scored dimension; keep it as a non-scored "comfort preferences" profile that informs delivery and is reviewed by humans. The plan engine's `sensory_need` rule (PROJECT.md §6, `p.sensory_sensitive()`) would then need to be driven by an explicit guardian/therapist input or a pattern flag a human confirms, rather than by a computed score — this is an inference for the project to decide.

### Gaps
- No study found that validates tablet setting choices (volume, animation, calm mode) against an established sensory measure in children of any age.
- No Pakistan-specific prevalence or cultural data on sensory differences in children found.
- Could not retrieve Ben-Sasson 2009 effect sizes or the SOR–ADHD relationship from full text.
- No research found on how often typical children toggle comfort settings out of novelty, which would be needed to set a baseline if a pattern flag were built.
