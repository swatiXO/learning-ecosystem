# Memory and Comprehension Tasks (follow_instructions, copy_the_pattern, story_and_questions, read_and_answer) for ages 5–12, Urdu/English, Android tablet

Scope note: dimensions are `working_memory`, `auditory_comprehension`, `reading_comprehension`. Not diagnostic; scores are tracked against the child's own baseline. No proprietary test items (WISC, CELF, NEPSY, WRAT, AWMA items) are reproduced; only generic paradigms and published findings. **Every numeric parameter below is a literature estimate (source given) or is explicitly marked as a design proposal / inference, not a finding.**

---

## Q1. follow_instructions: paradigm, validity, structure, scoring, age, ADHD/autism, verdict

### Takeaway
"Following instructions" (spoken action sequences such as "touch the red pencil, then pick up the blue ruler...", enacted by the child) is an established, non-proprietary research paradigm from Gathercole and colleagues, closely tied to verbal working memory in 5–11-year-olds. Children are reliably better at *enacting* than verbally repeating instructions, which makes the tablet "tap it out" format a good fit. Keep it, with tablet-specific design and partial-credit scoring.

### Cited Findings
- Paradigm origin: Gathercole, Durling, Evans, Jeffcock & Stone (2008), "Working memory abilities and children's performance in laboratory analogues of classroom activities", *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 22(8), 1019–37. In 5-year-olds, given spoken instructions like "touch the red pencil, then pick up the blue ruler and put it in the black box", children were substantially better at enacting than at verbally repeating; the authors attributed the enactment advantage to a motoric/spatial representation linking objects to movements — [Cambridge Centre summary / PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3901937); [search summary of Gathercole et al. 2008](https://portal.camide.cam.ac.uk/read_more/1174)
- Administration in this paradigm: testing started at one action and increased by one action per block; children listened while not allowed to touch objects, then performed the sequence at the end (literature procedure) — [PMC5529483 / Springer s13423-013-0471-7 summary](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-013-0471-7)
- Validity: Jaroslawska, Gathercole, Logie & Holmes (n = 42, ages 7–11) found verbal working memory was closely linked with all three following-instructions paradigms tested (a real 3-D-object task and two computerised virtual-school tasks), i.e. the construct holds for screen-based versions — [UEA repository](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/81797); [Memory & Cognition article](https://mijn.bsl.nl/article/10.3758/s13421-015-0579-2)
- Instruction following draws on both simple (storage) WM and complex (processing/executive) WM — [PMC4835523 summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4835523)
- Children with poor working memory show particular difficulty remembering and following classroom instructions — [Alloway/Gathercole work summary, Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1555279/verbal-and-visuospatial-short-term-and-working-memory-in-children-are-they-separable)
- WM structure: storage depends on domain-specific verbal and visuospatial resources, while processing draws on a common pool; all components in place by age 4 (Alloway, Gathercole & Pickering, ages 4–11) — [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1555279/verbal-and-visuospatial-short-term-and-working-memory-in-children-are-they-separable); three distinct-but-correlated WM factors fit from age 6 onward (Gathercole, Pickering, Ambridge & Wearing 2004, *Dev Psych* 40(2), 177–190, ages 4–15) — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/5942/)
- Scoring: all-or-nothing span scoring "shows significantly reduced reliability relative to partial-credit scoring" (citing Conway et al. 2005); Wells, Kofler et al. (n = 34 children with ADHD, ages 8–13) found that administering all trials (ignoring the discontinue rule) and counting items in correct serial position, rather than trials correct, improved digit-span-backward's construct and predictive validity, while traditional scores predicted neither criterion WM nor achievement — [Wells et al. 2018 manuscript, FSU](https://psy.fsu.edu/clc/Publications/Wells2018.pdf); [Conway et al. 2005, Psychon Bull Rev 12(5) 769–786](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758%2FBF03196772)

### Inferences
- Tablet design (proposal, derived from the paradigm above): a fixed array of ~6–9 coloured shapes/objects; spoken instruction plays while the board is inert (taps disabled), then a "go" cue; child taps in order. Lengths start at 2 actions (1 as a practice item), going up by 1. Keep the same object array across trials so only the verbal load changes.
- Grade difficulty by number of actions *and* by linguistic complexity (adjective+noun "red star"; later spatial/conditional words "before", "under", "if"). Keep these as separate, logged parameters so scoring can tell sequence length apart from vocabulary or syntax. For 5–7-year-olds and for bilingual children, vocabulary (colour/shape names in the instruction language) is a confound: check colour/shape names in a pre-task (receptive "tap the red one") and log failures as a language flag, not a WM score.
- Score: proportion of actions in correct serial position (partial credit) plus highest length with ≥1 fully correct trial. Map to 0–100 against the length range used (e.g. lengths 2–7). Confidence goes up with number of trials and agreement between the partial-credit and span estimates (proposal).
- Instruction delivery must be fixed recorded audio (fixed rate) rather than live agent speech, so that week-1 conditions are identical (consistent with PROJECT.md rule on week 1).
- ADHD/autism: the verbal-storage deficit in ADHD is smaller than the spatial one (see Q2 findings); autism-related differences here are more likely to show up through language comprehension of the instruction than through storage. Log instruction-repeat requests and early taps (impulsivity) separately.
- **Verdict: KEEP** (strong paradigm, enactment format suits tablet and young children).

### Gaps
- No published age norms (mean action span by year of age, 5–12) for the following-instructions paradigm were retrievable; the PMC full texts were behind a CAPTCHA. Trials-per-length and test-retest reliability for this specific paradigm were not found.
- No Urdu-language following-instructions study found.

## Q2. copy_the_pattern (Corsi / Simon-like visuospatial span): paradigm, reliability, forward vs backward, norms, ADHD, verdict

### Takeaway
Corsi block-tapping is the standard non-proprietary visuospatial span paradigm and has been validated on tablets. Typical procedure: start at length 2, two trials per length, stop after failures at a level. Visuospatial WM shows the largest ADHD group difference of any WM domain (d ≈ 0.85–1.06 in meta-analysis), so this task is both valid and sensitive for this app's population. Forward span suits 5–7-year-olds; backward span is better kept as an optional harder block for 8+. Keep it.

### Cited Findings
- The Corsi block-tapping task has been called the single most important nonverbal task in neuropsychological research and is widely used to measure visuospatial short-term/working memory — [Brunetti, Del Gatto & Delogu 2014, eCorsi, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00939/full)
- Tablet validity: eCorsi (n = 107 adults) produced average spans and error rates essentially analogous to the standardisation studies using the physical board. Default procedure: start at 2 items, 2 trials per length, end when errors exceed an admissible proportion. Forward span > backward span (younger adults 6.11 vs 5.29) — [eCorsi, PMC4151195](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4151195/)
- Because Corsi is adaptive, responses within a session are not independent, so Cronbach's α / split-half was not computed in that study. Internal-consistency reliability of an adaptive span needs a different approach (e.g. test-retest, or fixed-length blocks) — [eCorsi search summary / Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00939/full)
- Developmental norms: Farrell Pagulayan, Busch, Medina, Bartok & Krikorian (2006), *J Clin Exp Neuropsychol* 28(6), 1043–52, n = 340 children and young adults. Performance increased with age and education, with an upper developmental plateau in early adolescence (no differences between ages 14 and 21) — [Corsi norms summary / Wikipedia references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsi_block-tapping_test); [Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/714341521/DevelopmentaldataPMT)
- Italian normative study, n = 268 typically developing children aged 4–11: age (not sex) affected Corsi performance. From ~5.6 years children performed better on the standard Corsi than on the walking (large-scale) version — [search summary, univaq / Springer cognitive processing](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10339-006-0125-z)
- Children 6–10: clear age effects and reliable path-complexity effects within Corsi. Structured paths are easier than unstructured ones, with older children benefiting more from structure — [search summary](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/17470210701774200)
- Spatial span (CANTAB-type) in ages 6–18: children with ≥6 years of schooling outperformed those with <3 years — [Teixeira et al. 2011, Dementia & Neuropsychologia](https://scielo.br/j/dn/a/c9n7NwNwM5D7xdbZr8nxbMs/?lang=en)
- The backward Corsi was introduced by Isaacs & Vargha-Khadem (1989) in a developmental study — [search summary](https://conductscience.com/corsi-block-test)
- ADHD: Martinussen et al. (2005) meta-analysis (26 studies, 1997–2003): spatial central-executive WM effect size 1.06 (CI 0.72–1.39), greater than for verbal storage and verbal central-executive WM — [PubMed 15782085](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15782085/); [Elsevier Pure record](https://latam-cl.demo.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-working-memory-impairments-in-children-with-at)
- Later meta-analyses (Kasper, Alderson & Hudec 2012, *Clin Psychol Rev* 32(7) 605–617; Alderson et al. 2013, *Neuropsychology* 27(3) 287–302) found moderate overall effects across phonological and visuospatial WM, with moderators (e.g. task characteristics) explaining between-group differences — [Loughborough record](https://vufind.lboro.ac.uk/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1353987286); [Anales de Psicología citing Kasper 2012](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/view/analesps.34.1.289671)
- Forward and backward digit span load on a single short-term memory factor, separately from complex-span WM tasks; "a simple transformation of order" is argued to be insufficient to turn a storage task into a WM task — [Wells, Kofler et al. 2018](https://psy.fsu.edu/clc/Publications/Wells2018.pdf)

### Inferences
- Structure (proposal anchored on literature defaults): 9 tappable locations in an irregular layout (Corsi standard). Start at length 2 with 2 trials per length (eCorsi default). Continue while ≥1 of 2 trials at a length is correct; stop after both trials at a length fail. Add a practice length of 1–2 with feedback-free animation. To get a reliability-friendly score, use the Wells/Conway recommendation: give one extra length beyond the discontinue point, and score partial credit (items in correct position), not just span.
- Reliability ≥ .70: no child-specific reliability coefficient for tablet Corsi with N trials was found. Practical inference: 2 trials/length gives a coarse integer span, so averaging the span estimate over two administrations (e.g. day 1 and day 7 of week 1, which the schedule already repeats for some tasks) plus partial-credit scoring is the most defensible route to a stable baseline. Confidence should be low (e.g. ≤0.5) after one run.
- Forward vs backward: the literature shows backward is harder (adult means above). The Wells/Kofler argument says backward adds little construct beyond forward. For 5–7, forward only. For 8–12, an optional backward block can be added for ceiling headroom, but don't treat it as a different construct.
- No fail states: present lengths as "levels" with a neutral end ("great remembering!"); the stop rule is invisible to the child.
- Simon-like (coloured pads with tones) vs Corsi (identical blocks): tones and colours let children recode the sequence verbally ("red, blue..."), which mixes in verbal WM. To keep this task visuospatial and distinct from follow_instructions, use identical-looking locations with no colour/sound coding, or a uniform tone.
- **Verdict: KEEP** (strongest psychometric pedigree of the four; most ADHD-sensitive).

### Gaps
- **Mean Corsi span by year of age for 5–12 could not be retrieved** (Farrell Pagulayan 2006 and the Italian 4–11 norms tables were paywalled). Commonly cited figures (forward span roughly 3–4 at age 5 rising to ~5–6 by early adolescence) are unverified here and should be checked against the Farrell Pagulayan 2006 tables before setting floor/ceiling.
- Effect sizes for verbal storage in Martinussen 2005 and the exact Kasper 2012 phonological/visuospatial d values were not retrievable (PubMed blocked by cookie wall).
- No test-retest coefficient for Corsi in 5–12-year-olds found.
- No Corsi data from Pakistani children found.

## Q3. Do follow_instructions and copy_the_pattern overlap? Keep both?

### Takeaway
They tap separable components. Storage is domain-specific (verbal vs visuospatial) from early childhood, even though processing shares a common resource, and ADHD effects differ by modality. Keep both, but keep them clearly apart: no colour or name coding in copy_the_pattern, no spatial-sequence-only cues in follow_instructions.

### Cited Findings
- Storage aspects depend on domain-specific verbal and visuospatial resources, while the processing component draws on a common pool, in children aged 4–11 — [Alloway, Gathercole & Pickering, Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1555279/verbal-and-visuospatial-short-term-and-working-memory-in-children-are-they-separable)
- A three-factor (phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, central executive) model fits from age 6 — [Gathercole et al. 2004](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/5942/)
- Spatial WM shows a larger ADHD effect than verbal WM — [Martinussen et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15782085/)
- Following instructions recruits verbal WM, but enactment also builds a motor/spatial representation — [Gathercole et al. 2008 summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3901937)

### Inferences
- Both should feed `working_memory`. The extractor should keep sub-scores (`wm_verbal`, `wm_visuospatial`) in evidence jsonb so a verbal-vs-spatial discrepancy stays visible. That discrepancy is useful information for the plan engine. It is not a diagnostic label.
- follow_instructions also loads on `auditory_comprehension` (understanding the instruction words). Score it for WM only when vocabulary checks pass.

### Gaps
- No study found directly comparing a tablet following-instructions task with tablet Corsi in the same 5–12 sample.

## Q4. story_and_questions (listening comprehension): paradigm, structure, items for reliability, scoring, age, autism, localisation, verdict

### Takeaway
Listening comprehension of a short narrative with literal + inferential questions is the standard non-proprietary paradigm (EGRA uses one story plus 3 questions; research tests use ~19–30 items). A reliable estimate (≥ .70) needs on the order of 20–30 items. Over week 1 that means several short stories (e.g. 4–6 stories × 4–5 questions), not one story. Inferential questions are where autistic children diverge most, so keep the literal/inferential split. This is the only comprehension measure that works for non-readers, so it is essential for the 5–7 band. Keep it.

### Cited Findings
- Portuguese listening-comprehension tests (Santos, Viana, Ribeiro, Prieto, Brandão & Cadime 2015; grades 1–4; n = 1,042 narrative / 848 expository): 30 items per form, Rasch-scaled, reliability > .70 for all forms. Early versions suffered "a severe lack of difficult items" (ceiling) — [Spanish Journal of Psychology, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/spanish-journal-of-psychology/article/development-of-listening-comprehension-tests-with-narrative-and-expository-texts-for-portuguese-students/1CB8A3441312304564AA82C2771AE127)
- ICNA (Filiatrault-Veilleux & Desmarais 2023, English version of the French ÉCIR, ages 3–6): a 20-page picture story with a predictable story-grammar structure, 19 questions asked *within* the story targeting six causal-inference types (problem, internal response, goal, prediction, attempt, resolution), scored 0–3, about 15 minutes. Explicitly criterion-referenced and "not as a tool for identifying a disorder". Young children respond better when an adult asks the questions than the computer — [ICNA technical manual](https://lel.crires.ulaval.ca/cms/1691721704987-techical-manuel_icna_jan-2023.pdf)
- EGRA Sindh 2024 (grade 2, government schools): listening comprehension = enumerator reads a story aloud, then asks 3 questions. Urdu-medium mean 2.30/3 (SD 0.88), 5% zero scorers; Sindhi-medium 1.66/3 (SD 0.98), 14% zero; many students failed even literal questions — [EGRA Baseline Assessment Report Sindh 2024](https://rsusindh.gov.pk/rsusindh.gov.pk/contents/SELECT/EGRA_Baseline_Assessment_Report_Sindh.pdf)
- Autism, inferential narrative comprehension: in a study of 35 autistic 5–6-year-olds vs 32 TD peers listening to a picture-supported story, autistic children may have inferential-comprehension difficulties regardless of adequate word- and sentence-level structural language — [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/9be2d792-2ac7-4900-a514-01d5715d9bf4); [Autism & Developmental Language Impairments 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/23969415211035666)
- Within inferential questions, differences concentrated in thought and evaluative inferences; emotional inferences challenging — [UCM Revista de Logopedia summary](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/RLOG/en/article/view/100221)
- Brown, Oram-Cardy & Johnson (2013) meta-analysis (36 studies): autistic readers were significantly worse at comprehending highly social texts than less social texts — [IMFAR abstract](https://imfar.confex.com/imfar/2012/webprogram/Paper9964.html); [Springer/JADD record](https://antoniusacademie.mijn.bsl.nl/a-meta-analysis-of-the-reading-comprehension-skills-of-individua/554710)

### Inferences
- Structure (proposal): 4–6 stories across week 1, each 60–120 s of recorded narration (fixed voice and rate) with 2–4 static illustrations, followed by 4–5 picture-choice questions (3–4 options, tap to answer). Aim for 20–30 total items for a baseline (matching the 30-item / α > .70 benchmark). Questions after the story (not mid-story) test memory + comprehension; ICNA-style mid-story questions reduce memory load for 5–6-year-olds. Pick one approach and keep it fixed across versions.
- Question mix (proposal): about 40% literal and 60% inferential (causal: why did X happen, how does X feel, what will X do next). Tag every item `literal`/`inferential` and `social`/`non-social`. Report sub-scores, because the autism literature shows a social-vs-non-social and literal-vs-inferential split.
- Scoring: proportion correct overall → 0–100. Literal and inferential sub-proportions go into evidence. Confidence scales with items answered (e.g. near 0.3 at 5 items, rising toward 0.8 at ~25 items: proposal tied to the 30-item reliability benchmark). Rasch/IRT scaling (as in Santos et al.) lets stories of different difficulty be equated later.
- Avoid ceiling: the Portuguese tests found a lack of hard items. Include harder inferential items so 11–12-year-olds have headroom. Use age-banded story sets (5–7: 80–150 words, one character goal; 8–10: 150–250 words, two characters; 11–12: 250–400 words with a twist or a mental-state inference). Word counts are proposals; no Urdu-specific source found.
- Avoid passage-independent questions (answerable from general knowledge). Pilot each question by asking it *without* the story; see Q5 finding (Keenan et al.).
- Allow replay of the story? Replay improves fairness for ADHD attention lapses but changes the construct. Proposal: allow one replay, log it as a signal, and score first-attempt and post-replay separately.
- Localisation: recorded Urdu and English narration by native speakers. Pakistani settings (home, bazaar, school, cricket, Eid, animals, village/city). Avoid content tied to a particular religion or sect beyond widely shared festivals, and avoid stories that hinge on fear or loss for this population. Cross-check with the app's template-library rules (PROJECT.md §12). The Urdu-medium vs Sindhi-medium EGRA difference shows that the *language of the story* strongly moves scores, so language must be logged and pinned during baseline.
- **Verdict: KEEP** (the primary comprehension measure for all ages; must exist as the fallback for non-readers).

### Gaps
- No test-retest data for short tablet listening-comprehension tasks in 5–12-year-olds was found; reliability benchmarks are internal-consistency (α) from paper tests.
- No published Urdu listening-comprehension norms beyond EGRA's grade-2, 3-question subtask.
- Effect size for the autism inferential gap in the 5–6-year-old study was not retrievable (403).

## Q5. read_and_answer (reading comprehension): paradigm, validity confounds, items, autism/hyperlexia, verdict, and the 5–7 non-reader question

### Takeaway
Silent or oral passage reading followed by literal and inferential questions is the standard paradigm, but reading comprehension = decoding × language comprehension. Tests differ widely in how much each drives the score. Autism shows an average deficit (g ≈ −0.7) with large individual variation, plus a subgroup (6–21%) with hyperlexia, where decoding outruns comprehension. In Pakistan most 5–7-year-olds, and many older children, cannot read a grade-2 Urdu story, so the task will floor badly. Redesign: gate it by a short decoding check, offer it only to children who pass, and use story_and_questions as the comprehension estimate for everyone else.

### Cited Findings
- Keenan, Betjemann & Olson (2008), *Scientific Studies of Reading* 12(3) 281–300: popular reading-comprehension tests (GORT, QRI, WJ Passage Comprehension, PIAT) differ dramatically in how much of their variance comes from decoding vs listening comprehension, so they are not interchangeable — [CU Boulder record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_53767)
- Passage-independent items (answerable without reading) occur, especially in lower-level passages — [Perspectives on Language 2016 summary](https://www.onlinedigeditions.com/article/Assessing+the+Assessments%3A+Reading+Comprehension+Tests/2460781/298764/article.html)
- Autism meta-analysis (Brown, Oram-Cardy & Johnson 2013, *JADD* 43(4) 932–955; 36 studies): overall g = −0.7. Strongest predictors were semantic knowledge (57% of variance) and decoding (55%). Worse on highly social texts. "Having ASD alone does not predict reading comprehension deficits" — [IMFAR abstract](https://imfar.confex.com/imfar/2012/webprogram/Paper9964.html); [Behaviorist Book Club summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/a-meta-analysis-of-the-reading-comprehension-skills-of-individuals-on/)
- Hyperlexia: Ostrolenk et al. (2017) review (82 case reports, 22 group studies, 315 hyperlexic participants): reported prevalence of hyperlexia in autism ranges 6–21%, and 84% of published hyperlexic cases were autistic — [INSAR 2018 abstract](https://insar.confex.com/insar/2018/webprogram/Paper28551.html)
- For many autistic children, decoding/word recognition can be a relative strength while comprehension of the same text lags; accuracy/fluency-focused assessment can be misleadingly positive — [PMC9754047 / search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9754047)
- EGRA Sindh 2024 (grade 2) passage reading: 60-second timed oral reading of a ~60-word passage, then 5 untimed oral questions. Urdu-medium mean 19.25 correct words per minute (expected standard 50–80 CWPM); 34.81 CWPM excluding zero scorers. Comprehension mean 1.19/5; only 6% got all 5 right; **45% scored zero on fluency and 60% zero on comprehension** — [EGRA Sindh 2024](https://rsusindh.gov.pk/rsusindh.gov.pk/contents/SELECT/EGRA_Baseline_Assessment_Report_Sindh.pdf)

### Inferences
- Floor for 5–7: the EGRA and ASER figures (Q6) show the reading task would produce mostly zeros in this band and a large share of zeros up to ~age 10 in many districts. A zero-heavy distribution has no reliability and turns into a fail experience, which conflicts with rule #5 (no fail states).
- Redesign (proposal):
  1. A 30–60 s decoding probe (tap the word you hear among 3–4 printed words; read 5 short words, then a sentence), presented as a game. ASER-style levels (letter → word → sentence → story) set the entry point.
  2. Only children at sentence level or above get read_and_answer passages, levelled to their decoding level. Others get nothing extra; their `reading_comprehension` stays "insufficient evidence" (confidence ≈ 0), *not* a low score. This matters: a non-reader should not get a low `reading_comprehension` score that pushes the plan engine. Missing evidence ≠ low skill.
  3. Passages support optional read-aloud of words on tap. Log taps so decoding support is visible in evidence.
  4. Same literal/inferential and social/non-social tagging as story_and_questions. To separate decoding from comprehension, ask an equivalent listening version of a passage at a different difficulty level; a large listening–reading gap is the decoding signal, and reading ≫ listening suggests a hyperlexia-like profile. The app records skills, not labels (rule #1).
- Item count: same α > .70 benchmark suggests ~20–30 items in total. For readers, 3–5 short passages × 4–6 questions across week 1 (proposal).
- Script: Urdu passages in Nastaliq (the script children see in Pakistani schoolbooks) at a large font size. Diacritics (zer/zabar/pesh) are optional for beginner levels (see Q6 on diacritic transparency). English passages are offered separately, never mixed in one score.
- **Verdict: REDESIGN** (decoding-gated, levelled, reader-only; skip for non-readers in 5–7 and for any child below sentence level).

### Gaps
- No Urdu reading-comprehension tests with published reliability for primary-age children were found (only fluency scales such as URAS were mentioned).
- No source gives the share of urban, higher-SES Pakistani 5–7-year-olds (likely early adopters of a tablet app) who can read; ASER and EGRA mostly sample rural/government schools.

## Q6. Urdu/Pakistan localisation: ASER reading levels, Urdu readability, Nastaliq, bilingualism

### Takeaway
ASER Pakistan uses a 5-level oral reading ladder (beginner / letters / words / sentences / story), with the "story" set at grade-2 level. In 2023 only about half of grade-5 children nationally could read that grade-2 Urdu story, and about 8% of grade-3 children. No validated Urdu readability formula exists; levelling should follow ASER/EGRA-style criteria (passage length, word familiarity, sentence length) and graded open collections. Urdu script is visually complex and, without diacritics, orthographically opaque, which slows early reading compared with Hindi.

### Cited Findings
- ASER 2023: nationally 50% of grade-5 children could read a story in Urdu (provincial: Punjab 65.1%, AJK 76.1%, GB 60.5%, Balochistan 45.7%, KP 44.2%, Sindh 38.5%) — [Business Recorder](https://www.brecorder.com/news/amp/40232775); [stateofchildren subject-wise outcomes](https://stateofchildren.com/subject-wise-performance/)
- In rural areas, 49% of grade-5 children can read a grade-2-level story in Urdu/Siraiki — [ITA/ASER](https://itacec.org/?p=4193); [stateofchildren 2023 provisional](https://stateofchildren.com/?p=7336)
- At grade 3, only ~8% of children could read an Urdu story (ASER 2023, as reported) — [Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/amp/1824238); [stateofchildren](https://stateofchildren.com/report-raises-concerns-over-dip-in-childrens-learning-levels/)
- ASER methodology: a household-based oral assessment of children aged 3–16 (272,370 children; rural 151 districts, urban 123 districts). Children are tested on a grade-2-level Urdu/Sindhi story, an English sentence and arithmetic — [stateofchildren national report](https://stateofchildren.com/annual-status-of-education-pakistan-2023-national-report-urban-and-rural/)
- Note: the "8% of grade 3" figure is from news reporting of the 2023 report. Treat it as approximate; the ASER grade-3 metric may differ (e.g. story vs sentence) from the grade-5 metric.
- EGRA Sindh 2024, grade 2: expected Urdu ORF standard 50–80 CWPM vs observed mean 19.25 — [EGRA Sindh 2024](https://rsusindh.gov.pk/rsusindh.gov.pk/contents/SELECT/EGRA_Baseline_Assessment_Report_Sindh.pdf)
- Urdu readability: no Urdu-specific readability formula was found in the literature search. Readability formulas in general are often inaccurate for judging which age group can read a text and should support, not replace, judgement — [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/can-readability-formulas-be-used-successfully-gauge-difficulty-reading); [Malaysian J. Learning & Instruction](https://e-journal.uum.edu.my/index.php/mjli/article/view/7668)
- Graded open readers: StoryWeaver (Pratham Books) has 4 reading levels based on book length, vocabulary complexity and storyline, explicitly *not* age or grade. Urdu is among its languages. A child's level can differ across languages — [StoryWeaver re-levelling post](https://storyweaver.org.in/v0/blog_posts/19-re-levelling-stories-on-storyweaver); [StoryWeaver school libraries post](https://storyweaver.org.in/blog?page=15)
- Urdu orthography: Rao, Vaid, Srinivasan & Chen (2011) found words were named faster and more accurately in (transparent) Hindi than in (more opaque, graphemically complex) Urdu. Urdu's cursive script has many graphemes that share a shape and differ only in the number or position of dots. Urdu has a "dual orthography" depending on presence or absence of short-vowel diacritics — [Rao et al. 2011 PDF](https://oaktrust.library.tamu.edu/bitstream/1969.1/158732/1/Rao%2C%20Vaid%2C%20Srinivasan%20and%20Chen%202011.pdf); [search summary of Urdu orthography literature](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5574968/)
- Urdu is written in Nastaliq — [Frontiers in Education 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1572807/pdf)
- Bilingual Urdu–English children (Pakistan and Canada): kindergarten Urdu phonological awareness longitudinally predicted grade-1 Urdu word/nonword reading accuracy and fluency, and rapid automatised naming predicted fluency (U-PASS tele-assessment) — [ASHA LSHSS 2025](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2025_LSHSS-24-00131)

### Inferences
- The decoding gate (Q5) can mirror the ASER ladder (letter → word → sentence → story) without copying ASER items. ASER is a public citizen-led tool, but the app should write its own words and stories.
- Urdu passages for beginner levels: include diacritics (more transparent), use high-frequency vocabulary, short sentences, and a large Nastaliq font with generous line height (Nastaliq's diagonal baseline and dot clusters make small type harder; inference from the visual-complexity findings).
- Bilingual: Pakistani children often learn to read English (Roman script) and Urdu (Nastaliq) at the same time, and English-medium private schools may build English decoding earlier than Urdu. The decoding gate should run per language, and reading_comprehension evidence should store the language. Never combine Urdu and English reading into one baseline (consistent with StoryWeaver's "levels differ across languages").
- Story content: localised settings and names; avoid passage-independent cultural knowledge (e.g. a question answerable from knowing an Eid custom). This is a validity issue, not just a cultural one.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed Urdu readability formula or graded Urdu word-frequency list for children was found.
- No data on reading levels of urban private-school 5–7-year-olds specifically.
- The Urdu Reading Assessment Scale (URAS) for grade 5 fluency was mentioned in search results but its details weren't retrieved — [tehqeeqat.org](https://tehqeeqat.org/downloadpdf/2361).

## Q7. Cross-cutting: trials needed for reliability ≥ .70, forward vs backward, number of comprehension questions, 0–100 + confidence mapping, age banding, engagement, verdict summary

### Takeaway
Reliability comes mostly from (a) partial-credit rather than all-or-nothing span scoring, (b) going beyond the discontinue point, and (c) roughly 20–30 comprehension items. No source gave an exact "N trials → r = .70" figure for child span tasks, so week-1 baselines should combine two administrations and report confidence honestly.

### Cited Findings
- All-or-nothing span scoring has significantly lower reliability than partial-credit scoring; credit for items recalled correlates better with other measures — [Conway et al. 2005](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758%2FBF03196772); [Wells et al. 2018](https://psy.fsu.edu/clc/Publications/Wells2018.pdf)
- Administering all trials (ignoring discontinue) and counting items correct improved digit span backward's validity in 8–13-year-olds with ADHD — [Wells et al. 2018](https://psy.fsu.edu/clc/Publications/Wells2018.pdf)
- A "mean span" digit-span metric showed reduced variance and improved test-retest reliability compared with traditional span — [science.gov / Pham SRCLD 2021 poster](https://uwo.ca/fhs/lwm/files/srcld_poster/Pham_DigitSpan_SRCLD2021.pdf)
- Comprehension: 30 items gave α > .70 in grades 1–4 listening tests — [Santos et al. 2015](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/spanish-journal-of-psychology/article/development-of-listening-comprehension-tests-with-narrative-and-expository-texts-for-portuguese-students/1CB8A3441312304564AA82C2771AE127). The ICNA uses 19 inferential items (0–3 each) for ages 3–6 — [ICNA manual](https://lel.crires.ulaval.ca/cms/1691721704987-techical-manuel_icna_jan-2023.pdf). EGRA uses 3 (listening) and 5 (reading) items. EGRA is meant for population monitoring, which is too few items for an individual score — [EGRA Sindh 2024](https://rsusindh.gov.pk/rsusindh.gov.pk/contents/SELECT/EGRA_Baseline_Assessment_Report_Sindh.pdf)
- Visuospatial span plateaus in early adolescence (14–21 no difference), so 11–12-year-olds are near but not at the ceiling — [Farrell Pagulayan et al. 2006 summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsi_block-tapping_test)

### Inferences
- **0–100 mapping (proposal):** for span tasks, score = 100 × (partial-credit items correct ÷ items presented across a fixed length range for the age band). Store raw span and partial-credit proportion in evidence. For comprehension, score = 100 × proportion correct (later Rasch-equated). Since progress is measured against the child's own baseline, the 0–100 scale only needs to be monotonic and stable within a child; it doesn't need population norms.
- **Confidence 0–1 (proposal):** a function of items/trials (span: saturating near 2 full administrations; comprehension: saturating near 25–30 items), reduced when engagement flags fire (≥2 quits/skips, many very fast taps under ~300 ms, or replays). Engagement goes in the Learner State separately from skill (PROJECT.md §12 item 4).
- **Age bands (proposal):** 5–7: forward span only, follow-instructions lengths 1–5, stories 80–150 words with pictures, no reading task unless the gate is passed. 8–10: forward Corsi + optional backward, instructions up to 6–7 actions with spatial/temporal words, stories 150–250 words, reading for those at sentence level or above. 11–12: add backward and longer stories/passages with harder inferences for ceiling headroom. Start difficulty from the age band, but let staircases adapt so a child isn't stuck at a floor or ceiling. That fits rule #10: age band sets starting difficulty only.
- **Engagement / ADHD:** keep each block under ~3–4 minutes (proposal). Use neutral, effort-based feedback; never show "wrong". Log lapses (no response within N s) separately from errors, because ADHD WM performance is affected by attention lapses as well as capacity.
- **Verdict summary:** follow_instructions: KEEP. copy_the_pattern: KEEP (forward for all; backward optional 8+). story_and_questions: KEEP (core comprehension measure, all ages). read_and_answer: REDESIGN (decoding-gated, levelled, readers only; skip for non-readers, most of the 5–7 band in Pakistan). No merges recommended: the two WM tasks measure separable storage systems, and the two comprehension tasks should be compared with each other (listening vs reading gap), not merged.

### Gaps
- No source states the exact number of span trials required for r ≥ .70 in 5–12-year-olds. This needs direct piloting (test-retest on day 1 vs day 7 in the pilot).
- Child test-retest reliability for tablet Corsi and for following-instructions tasks was not found.
- No validation of any of these paradigms in Urdu-speaking Pakistani children was found other than EGRA/ASER population tools and the U-PASS phonological study.
